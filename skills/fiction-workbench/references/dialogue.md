# Dialogue That Develops

Read for what the speakers do to each other's understanding. Natural dialogue is selected speech, not a transcript padded with greetings, hesitations and gestures.

## Give the conversation somewhere to go

Start with a local reason to speak: arrange a pickup, tell a story, ask for help, enjoy being funny, invite someone to stay. Private motives may differ. A conversation need not conceal a secret, enact a power struggle or carry the novel's theme.

Track what each reply responds to. People can answer part of a question, misunderstand, pursue their own interest or change the subject, but the turn must have a reason. An oblique reply without a reason is just evasive writing. Sometimes the truthful, ordinary answer is strongest.

Let a new detail alter the next turn. If every line merely demonstrates traits already listed in the character files, change the conversational event. A decisive woman need not correct every sentence; a guarded man can tell a story expansively. Distinguish people through attention, assumptions, expertise, humour and what they will discuss—not assigned verbal tics.

## Rhythm and selection

Allow someone to finish a thought. Allow another person an unremarkable reply. Report routine conversation when the precise wording adds little. Keep direct speech where wording changes the encounter. Disagreement, affection and silence can each take different amounts of space.

Read the spoken lines alone. Does the conversation have movement without the narrator certifying every shift? If it collapses into interview, exposition or interchangeable repartee, repair that before adding gestures.

Then read it in scene. Restore actions that interfere with speech, expose a divided intention, change distance or attention, reveal a specific habit, or identify a speaker where genuinely needed. Remove habitual nodding, glancing, cup handling and wrist descriptions that merely fill the interval between quotations. “Said” is useful and repeatable; do not cycle through conspicuous synonyms.

Short replies are not automatically staccato. Repeated setup–correction–payoff exchanges across a whole scene are more suspect. Let a joke fail, go unanswered or be enjoyed without a competing punchline. Do not make all intimacy look like sparring.

## Diagnostic miniature: logistics

These invented examples demonstrate choices; they are not prose templates or book canon.

**Theme recitation:**

> “You always choose work over us.”
> “I'm afraid of losing my identity.”
> “You need to decide what matters.”

Both people have been assigned complete thematic positions. There is little to discover through the exchange.

**One possible ordinary exchange:**

> “Can you collect her on Thursday?”
> “I can't. That's the late one.”
> “I thought you changed it.”
> “I asked. They haven't got back to me.”
> “I have to tell the nursery today.”
>
> I opened the message again. My manager had replied before lunch.

The last fact changes our reading of the exchange. It earns that reflective sentence; extra jaw tightening would add little. This example suits concealment. Do not graft a lie onto an authorised scene of mutual affection. For companionship, the movement might instead be one person becoming interested in the other's story, enjoying a shared task or receiving an ordinary answer with unexpected relief.

## Dialogue in this project

Marco and Malia need independent interests, enjoyment and practical competence. Malia is not his therapist; Marco is not permanently evasive. Their speech does not have to disclose a diagnosis of their relationship. A child's question grows from the child's immediate concern, not the author's need for a haunting line. Use current ages and history; do not make siblings interchangeable.

For the later uncanny, distinguish an observation someone can share from the explanation only Marco believes. Another character can hear him accurately and still disagree. Avoid building his isolation by making everyone artificially dismissive or uniformly foolish.

## Revision questions

- What changes in this exchange, including pleasure or interest?
- Does a speaker know the fact they are using?
- Is the topic here because these people would pursue it, or because the outline needs it stated?
- Which line would this particular person struggle to say?
- Could reported speech remove routine turns and give the important ones room?
- Does the final exchange feel lived, or arranged to end on a polished answer?

Use the relevant questions; do not make every conversation satisfy every item.
